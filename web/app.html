Nursing Home Simulation

A simulated nursing home of 112 residents and their staff, followed through 200 winters of COVID-19 with and without far-UVC lamps. You can move the lamps and change every assumption.

Lamps clean the air a room shares, so in the model they act only on infections caught that way. How much infection spreads face to face or by touch has not been measured well, so results are given as a range.

Infection

Introduction

There are many replicable studies of how far-UVC light inactivates harmful pathogens in a lab. And these are great, but what an infection preventionist actually wants to know is "How can I best use these to prevent real infections in my building? Where should I install the lamps? Is it even worth putting them anywhere but common areas? What is the realistic magnitude of effect on real-world infections from installing far-UVC fixtures?"

It's hard to give specific, reliable numbers to these questions, because sicknesses are both infrequent and very random; existing human studies on far-UVC germicidal lamps are encouraging but have enormous error bars. And no study has attempted to answer what the appropriate arrangement of lamps is within a building.

So: this is a simulated nursing home of 112 residents and their staff, followed through 200 winters of COVID-19 with and without far-UVC lamps. You can move the lamps and change every assumption that went into the model, from "how much virus is transmitted face-to-face versus via room air" to "how quickly does far-UVC light kill this specific pathogen".

Presets

Common areas are every room except the bedrooms: dining rooms, activity room, wing lounges, therapy room, corridors, lobby, kitchen, office and staff break room. Each standard choice puts one lamp per 250 square feet of floor (23 square metres) in every room it covers, with at least one per room.

Lamps per room, by type of room

lamps:

Lamps are taken to be mounted high on the walls and angled down; a setting under Lamps points them straight across the room instead. How far their light travels through a room, and how much they must be dimmed to stay within exposure limits, come from Illuminate, the lamp-planning tool from OSLUV. A room takes at most four times one lamp per 250 square feet, which is as far as those figures go.

Bedrooms are a special case. Exposure limits for far-UVC assume a person is exposed for 8 hours in a day. A report for NHS Scotland notes this, says the light may need to be turned down where people are exposed for longer, and says far-UVC may not be appropriate where the same person is exposed around the clock, because so little is known about that use (Wood et al., NHS Scotland Assure, section 8.3.2). It does not mention bedrooms; they are the nearest case here. In the model, bedroom lamps are dimmed until the daily limit is met over the 20 hours a day residents spend in their rooms, which leaves them at about 40% of what the 8-hour check allows. A setting under Lamps changes this.

Limitations

Interpretation

These are results of a computer model: what follows from the assumptions above. They are not measurements of what lamps do in a real care home, and they say nothing about any one person's health. Hospital admissions are a fixed share of reported infections, taken from national reporting, so they add no evidence of their own.

We know of no completed randomised trial showing that far-UVC lamps reduce infections in care homes. The one randomised trial of ultraviolet air cleaning in care homes, which used a different kind of UV device in Australia, found no significant reduction in respiratory infections. The one study of far-UVC lamps in a care home was not randomised and was funded by a manufacturer. Whether these lamps reduce infections is an open question that this model cannot settle.

      Room-air spread and non-room-air spread: how the numbers were chosen

      The numbers

      In a nursing home, an estimated 70% of COVID-19 spread is room-air spread, which far-UVC lamps can act on. The remaining 30% is non-room-air spread: it reaches a person up close or by hands and surfaces, before the light can act. The optimistic case for non-room-air spread in a nursing home is 20% and the cautious case is 50%.

      Non-room-air spread is close to what studies call short-range spread plus spread by contact. The difference is that ordinary room air breathed while standing close counts as room-air spread here, because lamps do reach it.

      CaseNon-room-air spreadRoom-air spreadWhat it assumes

      Central30%70%They breathe out a middling amount in fine particles
      Cautious50%50%They breathe out very little in fine particles, so droplets dominate up close

      "Optimistic" means best for lamps, so it has the least non-room-air spread. These values come from a calculation and a judgment, not a measurement. No study has measured this split in a nursing home.

      Influenza has no separate figure. It uses the same non-room-air dose as COVID-19, and its split follows from how influenza behaves in the model.

      Two doses

      A person who shares space with someone infected takes in virus in two ways, and lamps can only reduce one of them.

      • Room-air dose. This is virus that has built up in the room's air over minutes and hours. Everyone in the room breathes it, near or far. Lamps and ventilation both remove it.
      • Non-room-air dose. This is everything else, and it arrives while the two people are close: air inhaled seconds after the other person exhaled it, droplets landing on the face, and virus picked up by contact with hands and surfaces. Far-UVC needs a few minutes to inactivate virus in air, so this dose arrives before the light can act.

      Cigarette smoke is a fair picture. Standing next to a smoker, you get the puff directly. Anywhere else in the room, you get the haze.

      non-room-air spread = non-room-air dose ÷ (non-room-air dose + room-air dose)

      Why the split depends on the room

      The split is set by the room as much as by the virus, because ventilation changes only the room-air dose. Halve the clean air and the room-air dose roughly doubles. The non-room-air dose stays the same.

      So in a stuffy room the haze dominates and non-room-air spread is a small share. In a well-ventilated room little haze is left, and the non-room-air dose is most of what remains.

      Ventilation is counted in air changes an hour: how many times an hour the room's air is replaced. The table shows a carer at a resident's bedside, using the central case from the next section.

      Estimates of droplet spread

      The three cases differ in one thing: how much of the non-room-air dose comes from droplets and contact (which we bundle into "non-room-air"). The inhaled part of the non-room-air dose can be estimated from airflow: near an infected person, the air carries about 9% more virus than the rest of a typical nursing-home bedroom. The droplet and contact part cannot be estimated that way. It comes from a published model, and it depends on how much virus an infected person breathes out in fine particles.

      The model's own chart shows how its split moves with that amount. The multiple in the last column is the droplet and contact dose divided by the inhaled part.

      The published model is a 2020 study of a healthcare worker caring for a patient (Jones 2020). It splits spread into contact, droplet and inhalation. The journal corrected the paper in 2021: the doses stood, but the step that turned dose into infection risk had been wrong. The corrected baseline is 60% droplet, 30% inhalation and 1.4% contact (Table 3 of the correction). The other rows are read off the correction's Figure 4, so they are approximate. A copy is one unit of the virus's genetic material, the usual way of counting virus in a sample.

      The droplet figure itself is partly an assumption. It depends on how much virus large droplets carry and how well virus that lands on the eyes, nose or mouth goes on to infect. A 2022 review co-written by the model's author lists both as open questions (Wilson et al. 2022).

      So how much viral spread can far-UVC light act on?

      Each case is what one part of that range gives when averaged over a whole home. A home is a mix of three settings: hands-on care in a bedroom, roommates sharing a bedroom, and dining or activity rooms. The calculation runs each setting across a range of room sizes, ventilation rates and time spent close, then combines them.

      Each range covers five different weightings of the three settings, from mostly care to mostly common rooms.

      • Optimistic, 20%. The 1,000 and 10,000 rows: infected people breathe out a lot of virus in fine particles.
      • Central, 30%. The 100 row.
      • Cautious, 50%. Between the baseline row and the top row: very little virus in fine particles, better ventilation, and a home where most contact is hands-on care.

      So far-UVC can act on about 80%, 70% and 50% of spread in the three cases.

      The calculation leans cautious in one respect. It gives lamps no credit for any air inhaled at short range, although some of the air near an infected person is old enough for the light to have acted on.

      Reconciling this with the Diamond Princess cruise ship study

      A separate model, fitted to a real outbreak, lands near 30% once it is adjusted for nursing-home ventilation. It is a study of the Diamond Princess cruise ship (Azimi et al. 2021), and it does not use the healthcare-worker model at all.

      Its median estimates were 36% short-range, 41% long-range and 21% surfaces. Short-range plus surfaces makes 57% non-room-air spread, on a ship at 9 to 12 air changes an hour.

      Applying the rule from above, the room-air dose grows as ventilation falls and the rest stays fixed. At 2 to 3.5 air changes an hour, the ship's 57% becomes 24% to 33%. Its higher mean estimate of 65% becomes 30% to 41%.

      By setting

      Non-room-air spread differs far more between settings than between cases, so a model that can take a value per setting should use these.

      SettingOptimisticCentralCautious
      Hands-on care in a bedroom30%40%60%
      Roommates sharing a bedroom3%4%9%
      Dining or activity room20%25%45%

      Roommates are low because they share air for many hours and spend little of that time face to face. Almost all of their dose comes through room air, which lamps reach.

      What is not settled

      Four things could move these numbers.

      • Where real patients sit on the fine-particle scale. This sets the whole gap between 20% and 50%. It can be measured, and breath-sampling studies exist (for example Coleman et al. 2022), but they have not been checked for this document. They would narrow the range more than anything else.
      • The 60% droplet figure is an average of very different simulations. In the middle half of the model's runs, droplets account for anywhere from 24% to 100%. A few high-dose runs carry most of the infections, and the correction's table does not show how those split.
      • The calculation is untested. It treats each room as evenly mixed, and no experiment has measured this split in a nursing home.
      • The same multiple is applied in every setting. The roommate and common-room droplet and contact doses were scaled by the same factor as hands-on care. That is an assumption.
