# How the room-air share of respiratory transmission has been measured

Compiled 2026-11-19. Literature review for the nursing-home far-UVC model. Companion to transmission.md (sections 4.3 and 5), far_uvc.md and influenza_anchor.md in this folder. Read those first; numbers verified there are reused here and marked "earlier pass".

Terms used throughout:
- Room-air share: the fraction of infections caught through well-mixed room air (long-range airborne). Lamps, ventilation and filtration act on this.
- Out-of-reach share: everything else, i.e. short-range airborne (the exhaled plume within about 1-2 m), large-droplet spray and contact/fomite. The model's input is this share. Model values now: COVID-19 40% central (25% favourable, 55% cautious); influenza 60% (35% to 85%).
- Read-status labels: FULL (I read the full text), ABSTRACT, SECONDARY (number seen only in a review, news piece or summary), EARLIER PASS (verified in an earlier file here), INFERRED (my own reasoning or arithmetic).

## Summary

Nobody has measured the room-air share directly. There are six indirect methods, and each answers a slightly different question:

1. Remove only the room-air route (upper-room UV, ventilation, HEPA) and see how much transmission falls. This gives a lower bound on the room-air share in that setting, but only if the intervention is strong and the study is unconfounded. The historical results range from near-complete protection (McLean's 1957-58 influenza ward: seroconversion 19% to 2%) to nothing (Westchester and MRC school trials). Modern COVID-era natural experiments found 35-80% reductions (Georgia schools; Marche schools). The two modern randomised care-home trials of air cleaning (AFRI-c HEPA, PETRA UV) were null, but they added little clean air. No randomised far-UVC trial in care homes has published results. PRiVII finished in May 2025 and its results are still unpublished.
2. Remove the other routes (hand hygiene, masks, face shields) and see what is left. Effects were small and imprecise. The EMIT influenza challenge study found almost no transmission even in the arm exposed to every route. Cowling's 2013 model of two household trials attributed about half of influenza transmission to aerosol (sensitivity range 25-85%), with no split between short and long range.
3. Human challenge and animal studies separate routes cleanly but in artificial settings. Rhinovirus spread by aerosol at a poker table, with 0 of 12 infected through fomites alone (Dick 1987). By hand-to-hand inoculation, 11 of 15 were infected (Gwaltney 1978). For TB, the guinea-pig studies show that upper-room UV achieves roughly 70-80% protection even when transmission is 100% airborne. That gives a calibration ceiling for every UV intervention study.
4. Outbreak investigations show that long-range room-air transmission happens and can dominate a single event (Skagit choir, Ningbo bus, a Dutch nursing-home ward with recirculated air). They cannot give a share across ordinary settings, because they are selected for being spectacular.
5. Epidemiological contrasts:
   - Outdoors versus indoors: under 10% of infections happen outdoors.
   - Train seating: the attack rate was 3.5% in adjacent seats versus 0.14% in other rows.
   - Ontario nursing-home crowding: a modelled 31% of infections would have been averted had all rooms been single.
   - UK contact-app data: 82% of transmissions followed more than 1 hour of exposure.
   These show both a strong distance gradient and a dominant role for long, shared-air exposures. They do not separate the two.
6. Mechanistic models give explicit splits, but each split mostly reflects its authors' parameter choices:
   - Azimi 2021 (Diamond Princess cruise ship): about 35% short-range, 35% long-range, 30% fomite.
   - Jones 2020 (healthcare workers giving care): droplet 35%, contact 8%, inhalation 57%.
   - Mizukoshi 2020 (close care): long-range 5-27%.
   - Nicas & Jones 2009 (influenza): respirable inhalation 17%.

Agencies (WHO 2024, CDC 2021, Public Health Ontario 2022) state that close range carries the highest risk and long range happens under poor ventilation. None gives a share. Public Health Ontario says the split "cannot be generalized".

My assessment (labelled as such below):
- COVID-19: 55% is defensible as a cautious end. It is not the most cautious value the literature permits. Care-specific mechanistic models put 60-90% of a carer's risk out of reach, and they cannot be ruled out. I would move the cautious end to about 65% and run 75% as a stress test. I would not go higher, because the ventilation natural experiments and nursing-home outbreak investigations point the other way, and no empirical study supports more than 75% out of reach.
- Influenza: 35-85% is honest about the uncertainty, but the 85% end rests almost entirely on Nicas & Jones' hand-contact assumptions. McLean and EMIT point lower. I would use central 50%, cautious 75%, and 85% only as a stress test.

## 1. Removing only the room-air route

This is the only method that speaks directly to the quantity the model needs, because upper-room UV, far-UVC and filtration act on exactly the route the model calls route A. The logic: if removing most of the room-air route removes a fraction f of transmission, then the room-air share is at least f and the out-of-reach share is at most 1 - f, in that setting.

Two calibration points limit how much can ever be expected. In Escombe's Lima guinea-pig study, where 100% of exposure was by air piped from a TB ward, upper-room UV prevented about 70% of infections (row 2). Mphaphlele's South African study gave about 74% uncorrected and 80% corrected, which the authors equate to about 24 extra air changes per hour (row 3). So even a strong room-air intervention against a purely airborne agent gives roughly 70-80%, because inactivation is not instantaneous and mixing is imperfect. An observed human reduction of 50% is therefore compatible with a room-air share around 60-70%, not 50%.

The historical school record is genuinely mixed. Wells' 1941 Pennsylvania measles study is the positive case, with about 24% of susceptibles infected in irradiated classrooms against about 59% in unirradiated ones, as recomputed by a secondary source; I did not open the 1942 paper. The Westchester community study (Downes 1950, read in full) found no benefit against acute respiratory illness, with Pleasantville's rates slightly higher than the control town's in all three years. The MRC's 1954 schools trial cut airborne bacteria without reducing minor infections. Perkins and Bahlke's New York rural-school studies are reported as null; I found no numbers. Jimenez/Peng 2022 gives the standard explanation: where UV worked, school was the only shared indoor space; where it failed, children shared buses and other rooms for hours. For a nursing home that explanation partly cuts in the model's favour, since residents' shared indoor space is mostly inside the building. But the lesson stands that a room-air intervention fails whenever untreated shared air remains elsewhere, which is the position of a far-UVC installation covering common rooms but not bedrooms, corridors and bathrooms.

McLean's Livermore ward (row 8) remains the strongest single datapoint for influenza: seroconversion 19% in control buildings, 18% among staff, 2% in the UV building, a reduction of about 90%. That sits at or above the Escombe ceiling, so at face value essentially all influenza transmission on that ward was through room air. It is a non-randomised 1950s comparison on a long-stay TB ward where patients shared air all day with little hands-on care, so it should not transfer directly to a nursing home.

For COVID-19 the best natural experiments are in schools. Buonanno et al. 2022 (row 9) compared 316 mechanically ventilated classrooms with about 10,100 naturally ventilated ones and reported a relative risk reduction of about 74% overall and about 80% at 10-14 L/s per student, with roughly 12-15% per additional litre per second. Gettings et al. 2021 (row 10) found 35% lower incidence with dilution alone and 48% with dilution plus filtration. Both are observational and both probably overstate: schools that upgraded ventilation likely did other things too. If even half the Marche effect is causal, the classroom room-air share exceeded 40%; at face value it exceeded 75%.

The care-home trials are the most relevant and the least informative. AFRI-c (row 13), a 91-home cluster randomised trial of portable HEPA, found no difference in any outcome; clean-air delivery was modest and adherence poor, with commentators estimating only 1-2 added air changes per hour and units mostly run on the lowest fan speed because of noise. PETRA (row 14) gave an incidence rate ratio of 0.91 (0.77-1.09) for flow-through UV units. The Danish far-UVC study (row 11) reported adjusted rate ratios of 0.12 and 0.69 in its two arms, which contradict each other. PRiVII, the one placebo-controlled far-UVC cluster randomised trial in long-term care, completed in May 2025 with no results posted as of the January 2026 registry update, and I found none. The practical conclusion: no care-home air-cleaning study has yet delivered a large verified dose of clean air together with a reliable infection endpoint, so the null results bound the effect of weak interventions rather than the room-air share.

## 2. Removing the other routes

Cowling's 2009 Hong Kong household trial (row 16) is the cleanest attempt to subtract the hand route. Overall odds ratios were 0.73 for hand hygiene and 0.89 for hand hygiene plus masks, neither significant; restricted to households where the intervention began within 36 hours of onset they were 0.54 and 0.46. Taken at face value, removing hands and some droplet spray roughly halved influenza transmission, leaving at most about half for aerosol of any range.

Cowling et al. 2013 (row 17) is the modelling step on top of those trials and the most-cited influenza number. In a three-route competing-risk model where hand hygiene is assumed to cut contact transmission by 50% and masks to cut droplet transmission by 50%, and where routes differ in their probability of producing fever plus cough, the aerosol share came out at about 51% in Hong Kong and 42% in Bangkok, with a sensitivity range of 25-85% and 25-75%. Two cautions for our use. The intervention efficacies were assumed, not estimated. And aerosol is defined by particle size, not distance; the discussion notes the greatest aerosol risk is probably close to the infected person. So "about half by aerosol" is an upper bound on the long-range share, not an estimate of it, and the out-of-reach share in those households was at least about 50%.

ResPECT (row 18) compared N95 respirators with medical masks among outpatient health workers across four seasons: 8.2% versus 7.2% laboratory-confirmed influenza, adjusted odds ratio 1.18 (0.95-1.45). Respirators filter fine aerosol far better, so the null is often read against a large aerosol share; the counter-reading is that both groups were also exposed in the community and wore devices only during patient care. I would not use it to move either end of the range.

## 3. Challenge and controlled-exposure studies

EMIT (row 19) is the only modern human-to-human transmission experiment for influenza. Fifty-two donors were inoculated and 42 infected; 75 recipients were exposed over four days, randomised to a face-shield plus hand-sanitiser arm (blocking droplet spray and contact but not aerosol) or to control. One serologically confirmed transmission occurred, in a control recipient: 2.9% versus 0%, against roughly 16% expected. The useful inferences are negative. Contact and droplet spray cannot have been efficient, because the arm exposed to every route produced almost nothing. And the authors' explanation for the much lower rate than in their pilot is that the follow-on study had single-pass mechanical ventilation at about 4 L/s per person, against a hotel room with recirculating air. That is an argument that ventilation suppresses influenza transmission, which is the lever far-UVC pulls, but it rests on a comparison of two very small studies.

Dick's 1987 rhinovirus poker experiments (row 20) remain the cleanest route separation in humans. Recipients wearing restraints preventing face-touching were infected 10 of 18, essentially the same as unrestrained recipients at 12 of 18, while a group handling freshly contaminated cards, chips and pencils and prompted to touch their faces every 15 minutes had 0 of 12 infected. I read these numbers in a secondary source quoting the paper. Against that, Gwaltney's deliberate hand-contamination experiments infected 11 of 15 (row 21); the reconciliation is that hands can transmit when loaded far beyond natural levels but rarely are. Note the limit for us: the players sat at conversational distance in one room for 12 hours, so "aerosol" there mixes short range with room-scale accumulation. It bounds the fomite share near zero and says nothing about the short/long split.

The SARS-CoV-2 human challenge (rows 22, 23) infected 18 of 34 volunteers but measured no person-to-person transmission. Its companion emissions study found viral RNA in air, breath, hand swabs and surface swabs, with two of eighteen participants emitting most of the airborne virus, supporting the heterogeneous emission the model already represents through quanta dispersion, but giving no route split. Hamster work (row 24) reports airborne exposure as more efficient than fomite exposure; I read only the title and a listing, so I report only the direction.

## 4. Outbreak investigations

These convinced the field that long-range transmission exists. They are selected for being surprising, so they cannot estimate a share across ordinary settings; using them that way would be the central error in this literature.

The Skagit choir (row 26) infected most of a 61-person room at about 0.7 air changes per hour, with no spatial pattern by quadrant and nobody seated within 3 m in front of the index case. The Guangzhou restaurant (row 25) infected 10 people across three tables in one recirculating air stream while 68 patrons at 15 other tables and all staff were spared, which shows that long range in practice often means within one air stream, not throughout the room. The Ningbo buses (row 27) gave 34.3% on the bus carrying the index case against 0% on the other, with high-risk seats at 42.4% against 26.5%, a difference that was not significant.

Two nursing-home investigations matter most. De Man et al. 2021 (row 30) describes a Dutch ward where 81% of residents and 50% of staff were infected within six days while six other wards had zero cases among 95 residents and 106 staff; the outbreak ward had a system admitting outside air only above a CO2 threshold. That is as close as the literature gets to a natural experiment for room air in a nursing home, and it points to room air carrying nearly the whole outbreak when ventilation fails. The Belgian investigation (row 31) is more informative on the share, because it tested distance explicitly: among event attendees, infection was not associated with distance to the index volunteer, with mask wearing or with direct contact. Had the out-of-reach share dominated at that event, a distance gradient should have appeared. Against that, the unit whose residents stayed in their rooms still reached 52.5%, showing how much spread happened afterwards by routes the event analysis could not see.

The Maastricht cohort of 60 Dutch long-term care facilities (row 33) found mechanical air recirculation significantly associated with higher outbreak odds in the classic-variant period, alongside ward size, psychogeriatric care and staff movement; no effect sizes appeared in the abstract I read.

## 5. Epidemiological contrasts

The indoor/outdoor contrast (row 35) puts under 10% of reported infections outdoors, with indoor odds 18.7 times outdoor. This is often quoted for airborne transmission, and Li 2021 leans on it, since droplets and fomites should not care whether you are outdoors. But it does not separate short from long range, because an exhaled jet is also diluted faster outdoors.

The seating data give the best distance gradients. On Chinese high-speed trains (row 28) the attack rate was 3.5% in adjacent seats, 1.5% in the same row and 0.14% in other rows, with the per-hour increase an order of magnitude steeper for adjacent seats. Taken literally, infections outside the index row were a substantial minority of all infections in that setting, but a train is well ventilated and exposures were short, which favours the near field.

The UK contact-app analysis (row 36) is the largest behavioural dataset: transmissions had a median exposure of 6 hours, 82% exceeded an hour, household contacts were 6% of contacts but about 40% of transmissions, and the authors state that a long time at greater distance carried risk similar to shorter times up close, with duration the stronger predictor. For a nursing home, where residents share day and dining rooms for hours, this argues against a very high out-of-reach share: the exposures producing most infections are the long shared-air kind that room-air cleaning reduces.

The Ontario crowding study (row 34) is the nursing-home-specific contrast: 9.7% cumulative incidence in crowded homes against 4.5% in homes with mainly single rooms, with a modelled 31.4% of infections averted had all shared rooms been single. Room sharing bundles roommate close contact with shared bedroom air, so it does not split the routes, but it caps how much of nursing-home transmission is attributable to the bedroom at all: roughly a third. Singapore contact tracing (row 37) found 5.9% among household contacts against 1.3% for work and social contacts, with sharing a bedroom and talking for 30 minutes or more both associated with transmission; I did not see the adjusted effect sizes.

## 6. Mechanistic and risk-assessment models

These are the only sources producing an explicit number, and the weakest evidence, because the number largely follows from the inputs.

Azimi 2021 (row 38) is the most-cited COVID-19 split: roughly 35% short-range, 35% long-range and 30% fomite on the Diamond Princess, with particles under 10 µm responsible for over half of transmission. Out of reach would be about 65%. Two features make it a poor template for a nursing home: passengers spent much of the outbreak confined to cabins with their own household, inflating close contact, and the 30% fomite share is far above what the surface literature now supports.

Jones 2020 (row 39) gives inhalation 57%, droplet spray 35% and direct contact 8% for an unprotected worker giving care. Since the near-field part of that 57% is also out of reach, the implied out-of-reach share is at least 43% and could approach 80%.

Mizukoshi 2020 (row 40) is the most pessimistic published estimate for a care setting and the study that most constrains the cautious end. Modelling nurse-patient care at 0.6 m in a 22.7 m³ room at 2 air changes per hour, it puts long-range respirable exposure at 5% at a saliva load of 10^7 PFU/mL and 18-27% at 10^8, short-range inspirable at 4-10%, and droplet spray and contact as the remainder, so out of reach is 73-95%. It is driven by droplet-spray parameterisation and a facing probability of 0.013 per care episode.

Nicas & Jones 2009 (row 41) is the influenza analogue: hand contact 31% (27-58%), respirable inhalation 17%, droplet spray 52%, with hand contact rising to 93% if upper and lower respiratory doses are assumed equally infectious. The authors state the answer turns on an unknown dose-response ratio. This is the sole basis for an 85% cautious end for influenza.

The reviews supply the frame rather than numbers. Wang et al. 2021 state that transmission is dominated by short-range aerosol inhalation at most distances within 2 m, that droplets dominate only inside about 0.2 m when talking, and that fomite transmission is far less efficient. Li 2021 argues long-range airborne is a continuation of the short-range route that matters when ventilation is poor, with the jet diluted about 24-fold by 1.6 m. Jimenez/Peng 2022 explains the historical record, in particular Chapin's conflation of proximity with droplet spray and the dilution of the school UV studies by untreated shared spaces.

One structural point belongs in the model's documentation. These splits are not constants of the pathogen. Because the near-field jet excess does not depend on ventilation while the far-field term does, the out-of-reach share rises mechanically as room air gets cleaner and falls as it gets dirtier. A nursing home with a recirculating or under-ventilated day room sits near the de Man end; the same home well ventilated sits nearer the Mizukoshi end. No single number is right for both.

## 7. Agency and expert positions

The WHO-led 2024 terminology consultation (row 45) defines airborne transmission as occurring at any distance, separates direct deposition (short, semi-ballistic) and contact from "through the air" transmission, and says different pathogens have different predominant or mixed modes and that quantifying the predominant mode remains challenging. It gives no proportions for any pathogen.

The CDC's May 2021 science brief (row 46) states that risk is greatest within three to six feet, that infection beyond six feet is less likely but repeatedly documented in enclosed spaces with inadequate ventilation, with exertion or raised voices, usually after more than 15 minutes of exposure, and that contaminated surfaces do not contribute substantially. Public Health Ontario's 2022 review (row 47) says plainly that the short/long-range split cannot be generalized, while noting nursing-home outbreaks attributed to long-range spread.

None of the three gives a number, and that is itself a finding: the model's most influential input is one that no agency is willing to quantify.

## Assessment (my own judgement, not a literature finding)

SARS-CoV-2 in a nursing home.

The empirical lower bounds on the room-air share come from interventions. Correcting for the 70-80% ceiling from the guinea-pig UV studies, the Marche school result implies a room-air share near or above 75% in a classroom if causal; Georgia implies at least 35-50%; the de Man ward implies something close to 100% during that outbreak. The upper bounds on the room-air share come from mechanistic care models: Mizukoshi puts it at 5-27%, Jones at roughly 20-40% depending on the unpublished near/far split.

These do not overlap, because they describe different exposures. Classroom and ward-air studies describe people sharing a room for hours without touching. Mizukoshi describes a nurse leaning over a patient. A nursing home is both: a resident in a day room for six hours, and an aide at 0.5 m for twenty minutes of feeding. The share depends on how much of a resident's exposure comes from each, which is exactly what an agent-based model should compute, so the share is better reported as an output under scenarios than fixed as an input.

On the current settings:
- Central 40% is well placed, between Azimi's 65% and the 25% or less implied by the ventilation natural experiments, nearer the latter, which I think is right for a setting with long shared-room occupancy.
- Favourable 25% is defensible: roughly what Marche and Georgia imply at face value, and roughly what the de Man ward implies.
- Cautious 55% is defensible but is not the most cautious value the literature permits. The studies that most constrain this end are Mizukoshi 2020 (73-95% out of reach for a care episode), Jones 2020 (43% or more, plausibly 70% or more once near-field inhalation is counted) and Azimi 2021 (about 65%). Three independent mechanistic analyses sit at or above 55%. None is an observation, but together they mean 55% should not be presented as the pessimistic extreme.

Recommendation: raise the cautious end to about 65%, which is Azimi's value and near the middle of Jones' plausible range, and add a 75% stress test labelled as the care-dominated, Mizukoshi-style scenario. I would not go above 75%. Doing so requires believing Mizukoshi's droplet-spray parameterisation, which has no observational support, and it runs against the Ferretti duration data, the de Man ward, the Belgian event analysis that found no distance gradient, and the school ventilation studies. The published far-UVC and HEPA trials in care homes cannot settle it either way: they were null, but they delivered little clean air with poor adherence, so they bound the effect of weak interventions rather than the share.

Influenza.

The evidence is thinner and more contradictory. Cowling 2013 puts aerosol of any range at about half of household transmission (sensitivity 25-85%), which makes about 50% a floor for the out-of-reach share rather than a ceiling. Cowling 2009 read directly says removing hands halved transmission at best. McLean says the room-air share was about 90%, implying out of reach near 10%. Nicas & Jones say 83% or more. These cannot all be right, and the spread is wider than for COVID-19 because the hand route for influenza is less well excluded.

My view: central 60% is too high. It is pulled up by Nicas & Jones, whose authors say the result depends on an unverifiable dose-response assumption, and it sits above the Cowling household estimate and far above McLean. I would use central 50%, cautious 75%, favourable 25%, and keep 85% only as a labelled stress test tied explicitly to the equal-infectivity assumption. Applied consistently, influenza's central value should be a little above COVID-19's (more hand transmission, similar aerosol physics), not twenty points above.

Where the evidence cannot settle it.

Three gaps are structural rather than fixable by more reading.
1. No study has measured the short-range versus long-range split directly for any respiratory virus in any setting. Every number here is either an intervention effect reinterpreted as a share or a model output. The one design that could separate them, exposing people at controlled distances under controlled ventilation, has been done only at EMIT's scale, which produced one transmission event.
2. The split is not a property of the pathogen. It depends on ventilation, room volume, occupancy, activity and the amount of hands-on care, all of which vary between homes and between rooms in one home. Measuring it perfectly in one facility would still not transfer.
3. The design that would settle it for our purposes, a well-dosed randomised trial of room-air cleaning in long-term care with a verified infection endpoint, has not reported. PRiVII finished in May 2025 and its results are not public.

For the write-up: present the out-of-reach share as the model's dominant uncertainty, give the cautious end as roughly 65-75% for COVID-19 rather than 55%, state plainly that it has never been measured, and show far-UVC benefit as a curve across the range rather than a point estimate.
